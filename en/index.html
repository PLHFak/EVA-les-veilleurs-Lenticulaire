Calculation note of 9 October 2026

Lenticular sheet between two panes: 45 °C estimated at 35 °C air

This note evaluates by calculation the temperature of a transparent lenticular sheet of 2 × 1.8 m placed between two panes, on the sea wall promenade at Lombardsijde. For a day at 35 °C with no wind, the model gives 45 °C as a sealed sandwich and 47 °C with ventilated air gaps. The continuous service temperature of an extruded PMMA is 70 °C.

The fear of overheating is borne out for a printed lenticular sheet: with a dark image, the same model gives 70 to 105 °C. Unprinted, the absorbed energy is about seven times lower. These results remain an evaluation, to be confirmed by an instrumented mock-up.

In this evaluation, the sealed sandwich comes out better on most criteria, but it depends entirely on the quality of its seal. Temperatures are given for two cases: the site day (heatwave at 35 °C, orientation of the sea wall) and a more severe envelope case (800 W/m² at the same time as 35 °C).

Where the heat goes in each design

In A, the lenticular sheet touches both panes. Its heat passes by conduction into the glass, which the wind cools over 7.2 m². In B, the air in the gap insulates the lenticular sheet from the panes and carries almost nothing away.

Steady-state balance

Each layer receives its absorbed flux \(S\) and exchanges it with its neighbours. On each outer face, convection by the wind \(v\) and radiation to the surroundings (temperatures in kelvin):

\[q_{ext} = h_c \,(T_s - T_a) + \varepsilon \sigma \left( T_s^4 - T_a^4 \right), \qquad h_c = 4 + 4v\]

Between the lenticular sheet and each pane, conduction in the air of thickness \(\delta\) and radiation in parallel:

\[h_{air} = \frac{\lambda_{air}}{\delta} + h_r, \qquad h_r = \frac{4 \sigma T_m^3}{\dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_1} + \dfrac{1}{\varepsilon_2} - 1} \approx 6.5 \ \mathrm{W/m^2K}\]

Hand check for A in the envelope case, no wind, with all the heat leaving through the two faces:

\[\Delta T \approx \frac{S_{total}}{2\,(h_c + h_r)} = \frac{255}{2 \times (4 + 6.3)} = 12.4 \ \mathrm{K} \;\Rightarrow\; T \approx 47 \ ^\circ\mathrm{C}\]

The full model gives 49 °C at the lenticular sheet, with the panes at 48 °C. Estimated accuracy: ± 3 K.

Thermal inertia and sensitivity

The panel weighs 157 kg (144 kg of glass, 13 kg of PMMA) and stores 123 kJ/K. Its time constant is 30 min with no wind and 11 min at 4 m/s: equilibrium is reached in less than an hour, so inertia is not enough to flatten the peak.

If the lenticular sheet absorbed 20% of the sunlight (dirt, yellowing, tinted film), it would reach 54 °C in A and 59 °C in B in the envelope case. At 40%, 62 °C in A and 72 °C in B. An air clearance of 1 mm per side in A costs 1 K.

Natural ventilation: share of heat removed estimated at 5–8%

In a 10 mm gap, the calculated stack effect moves 9 to 14 m³/h. The air takes on the temperature of the walls within the first 15 centimetres, then rises without removing anything more.

The driving force is the density difference between the air in the gap and the outside air, helped or opposed by the wind:

\[\Delta p_{tirage} = \rho \, g \, H \, \frac{\bar{T}_{lame} - T_a}{\bar{T}_{lame}}, \qquad \Delta p_{vent} = \Delta C_p \, \frac{\rho v^2}{2}\]

It is used up by laminar friction between two planes a distance \(b\) apart and by the inlet and outlet openings, of relative cross-section \(\varphi\):

\[\Delta p_{frottement} = \frac{12 \, \mu \, H \, u}{b^2}, \qquad \Delta p_{orifices} = \frac{\rho u^2}{2} \left[ \frac{1}{(C_d \varphi_e)^2} + \frac{1}{(C_d \varphi_s)^2} \right]\]

Exchange between the air and the walls, and height after which the air is saturated with heat:

\[h_{lame} = \frac{Nu \, \lambda_{air}}{2b} = \frac{7.54 \times 0.0271}{0.020} = 10.2 \ \mathrm{W/m^2K}, \qquad z^* = \frac{\rho \, u \, b \, c_p}{2 \, h_{lame}} \approx 5.5 \ \mathrm{cm}\]

Dew point: threshold calculated between 76 and 94% humidity

On a clear night, the calculation gives a glass 1 to 4 K colder than the air. In B, dew can then also form on the inside, on the lens side. In A, the desiccant is there to prevent it.

Dew point

Magnus formula, \(T\) in °C, relative humidity \(HR\) between 0 and 1:

\[p_{sat}(T) = 611.2 \, \exp\!\left( \frac{17.62 \, T}{243.12 + T} \right) \ \mathrm{Pa}\]
\[T_r = \frac{243.12 \, \gamma}{17.62 - \gamma}, \qquad \gamma = \ln(HR) + \frac{17.62 \, T}{243.12 + T}\]

At 15 °C and 90% humidity, the dew point is 13.4 °C: the glass only needs to be 1.6 K colder than the air.

Glass temperature at night

A vertical face sees half sky and half ground. It loses by radiation to the clear sky what the air and the ground give back to it (temperatures in kelvin, \(T_r\) in °C in the sky emissivity):

\[h_c \,(T_a - T_s) + \tfrac{1}{2} \varepsilon \sigma \left( T_a^4 - T_s^4 \right) = \tfrac{1}{2} \varepsilon \sigma \left( T_s^4 - T_{ciel}^4 \right)\]
\[T_{ciel} = T_a \, \varepsilon_{ciel}^{1/4}, \qquad \varepsilon_{ciel} = 0.711 + 0.56 \, \frac{T_r}{100} + 0.73 \left( \frac{T_r}{100} \right)^2\]

On a night at 15 °C and 85% humidity, the clear sky radiates like a body at −1 °C. The glass drops to 11.0 °C with no wind and to 13.6 °C at 4 m/s.

Consequences for each design

  • Outer faces: same dew in A and in B. It dries in the morning and can be cleaned off.
  • B, inner faces: the cavity air is outside air and the panes are as cold inside as outside. Dew settles facing the lenses. Mist and sea spray enter through the openings.
  • A, sealed cavity: the desiccant holds the internal dew point well below 0 °C. No condensation as long as it is not saturated.
  • A, shower on a hot panel: if the sheet has not been dried (equilibrium at 50% humidity), the air in the film at 45 °C has a dew point of 32 °C. A pane suddenly cooled to 20 °C may fog for a few minutes. Sheet dried to 10%: dew point 6 °C, no risk.

Moisture: water estimated in the plastic and through the seal

The enclosed air contains only 0.02 g of water. The plastic would contain about 77 g, and the seal would let through 1 to 24 g per year depending on the sealant. In this estimate, it is the choice of seal that weighs most on service life.

Drying time of the centre of the sheet by the desiccant in the frame

The vapour has to diffuse over 0.9 m in an air film of 0.15 mm, while the sheet contains 29,000 times more water than this film. Characteristic time:

\[\tau = \frac{4 L^2 R}{\pi^2 D_v} = \frac{4 \times 0.9^2 \times 29{,}000}{\pi^2 \times 2.5 \cdot 10^{-5}} \approx 3.8 \cdot 10^{8} \ \mathrm{s} \approx 12 \ \mathrm{years}\]

On this timescale, it is better to dry the sheet before assembly. Through its 3 mm, via both faces, \(\tau = e^2 / (\pi^2 D)\) is about 3 weeks at 23 °C and 2 days at 60 °C. The diffusion coefficients of water in PMMA (5·10⁻¹³ and 5·10⁻¹² m²/s) are orders of magnitude, to be validated with the manufacturer of the lenticular sheet.

UV balance: less than 1% transmitted, according to the laminated glass data sheets

Summary

  • Both sides are protected, since both panes are laminated. The edges are covered by the frame.
  • The heat balance does not change. The UV that is stopped becomes heat in the interlayer. It is already counted in the 11% absorption of the glass, derived from the measured solar factor.
  • The 380 to 400 nm band is not quantified in the data sheets. It accounts for 30% of the UV energy. Kuraray states that a standard PVB stops almost everything up to about 400 nm. The glass supplier should be asked for the spectral curve of its interlayer.
  • An interlayer with a guaranteed cut-off exists if the curve is not sufficient (Trosifol UV Extra Protect, advertised as stopping all UV). Its thermal effect is negligible: less than 1% additional absorption.
  • What is at stake depends on the material. A standard PMMA itself absorbs UV and resists yellowing well. PETG or polystyrene is more sensitive to it (general assessment, to be checked on the data sheet of the lenticular sheet).

Thermal expansion: calculated difference of ± 3.7 mm over 2 m

PMMA expands eight times more than glass. Between −10 °C and +50 °C, the calculated difference reaches ± 3.7 mm over 2 m. In this evaluation, it is a more severe design constraint than temperature.

  • The sheet must be hung from the top and free elsewhere, with at least 5 mm of clearance on each edge. Clamped or bonded, it risks buckling and degrading the lenticular effect.
  • In winter, a perfectly sealed cavity goes into negative pressure (up to 10 kPa for −30 K). The panes press against the sheet and hinder its contraction.
  • A capillary pressure-equalisation tube opening into the desiccant balances the pressure. It costs only 0.7 g of water per year.

Preferred option at this stage: the sealed sandwich, to be validated by a mock-up

If the evaluation is confirmed, the sandwich would be built like an insulating glass unit with a very thin gap. The conditions below follow from the preceding calculations.

  1. Edge seal with butyl (PIB) barrier, backed by a retaining sealant. Neither silicone alone nor a dry EPDM gasket.
  2. 3A molecular sieve, at least 500 g distributed in the frame, preferably in a replaceable cartridge. Silica gel loses capacity when heated and is less suitable.
  3. Oven pre-drying of the sheet before assembly, of the order of 48 h at 60 °C, and assembly in a dry workshop.
  4. Sheet hung from the top, free elsewhere, 5 mm of clearance per edge, air films as thin as possible.
  5. Capillary pressure-equalisation tube, opening into the desiccant.
  6. Low-iron laminated glass 44.2 on both sides, with the UV curve of the interlayer provided by the glass supplier.
  7. Mock-up of 50 × 50 cm with a temperature and humidity probe, exposed on site for one month, before starting fabrication.

Assumptions to be confirmed, which may change the figures

  • Exact material and thickness of the lenticular sheet. PETG or polystyrene withstands heat less well than PMMA.
  • Exact azimuth of the sea wall at the location of the artwork (56° assumed).
  • Reference of the 44.2 glass, its EN 410 values and the spectral curve of its interlayer.
  • Frame detail: type of seal, location and mass of the desiccant.
  • RMI humidity and wind normals for the coast, and annual solar irradiation on PVGIS.
  • Focal length of the lenses: check that it remains of the order of the sheet thickness, to rule out any concentration of sunlight at a distance.
